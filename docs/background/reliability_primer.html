3. The reliability concepts needed#

Reading time

Ten minutes. This is the minimum needed to follow the Eurocode study; the lessons (Lesson 1 — Random variables and characteristic values and Lesson 2 — The reliability index and FORM) treat the same material with code and exercises, and the compendium [Köhler, 2026] or [Melchers and Beck, 2018] give the full account.

3.1. Limit state, failure probability, reliability index#

A failure mode is described by a limit state function \(g(\mathbf{x})\) of the basic variables \(\mathbf{x}\) — strengths, loads, model uncertainties — with \(g \le 0\) meaning failure. The probability of failure is

\[ p_f = P\big(g(\mathbf{X}) \le 0\big) . \]

For a linear \(g\) of normally distributed variables, the safety margin \(M = g(\mathbf{X})\) is normal and \(p_f = \Phi(-\beta)\) with \(\beta = \mu_M / \sigma_M\) — the reliability index. In general \(\beta\) is simply defined through \(p_f\),

\[ \beta = -\Phi^{-1}(p_f), \]

and is the quantity the codes prescribe: EN 1990 asks for \(\beta = 3.8\) for a 50-year reference period in the ordinary consequence class, which corresponds to \(p_f \approx 7 \cdot 10^{-5}\).

3.2. FORM: the design point and the sensitivity factors#

Each basic variable is mapped to a standard normal variable, \(x_i = F_{X_i}^{-1}\big(\Phi(u_i)\big)\), so that the problem lives in a space where the probability density is rotationally symmetric and decays with the distance from the origin. The first-order reliability method (FORM) [Hasofer and Lind, 1974, Rackwitz and Fiessler, 1978] finds the point of the failure surface \(g(\mathbf{u}) = 0\) closest to the origin — the design point \(\mathbf{u}^*\) — and takes its distance as the reliability index:

\[ \beta = \|\mathbf{u}^*\|, \qquad p_f \approx \Phi(-\beta). \]

The approximation replaces the failure surface by its tangent plane at the design point. It is exact for a linear surface and remains very good for the smooth, mildly curved surfaces of code calibration; the error can be checked with a second-order correction (SORM, [Breitung, 1984]) or with sampling around the design point, which is what the verification pages do.

The unit vector \(\boldsymbol{\alpha} = \mathbf{u}^* / \beta\) carries the sensitivity factors: \(\alpha_i^2\) is the share of variable \(i\) in the total uncertainty at the design point, and \(\sum \alpha_i^2 = 1\). Load variables have positive \(\alpha\) (a larger load brings failure closer), resistance variables negative. The design values are the coordinates of the design point mapped back, \(x_{i,d} = F_{X_i}^{-1}\big(\Phi(\alpha_i \beta)\big)\) — which is the relation the design value method of the previous chapter generalises with fixed \(\alpha\).

3.3. Characteristic values and partial factors#

The code works with one characteristic value per variable — a specified fractile: 5 % for material strengths, the mean (50 %) for self-weight, the 98 % fractile of the annual maximum for climatic actions — and a partial factor that moves it towards the design value, \(x_d = x_k \gamma\) for a load, \(x_d = x_k / \gamma\) for a resistance. A design made with the code provides a certain resistance; FORM on the limit state function then tells which \(\beta\) that design actually reaches. Calibration closes the loop by choosing the \(\gamma\) such that the \(\beta\)’s come out as intended.

3.4. Normalised variables#

In the Eurocode model every variable has mean 1 — the absolute magnitude of a strength or a load does not matter for the comparison, only its coefficient of variation (CoV), its distribution family and its characteristic value as a ratio to the mean. A resistance is then described by three numbers (family, CoV, \(x_k\)), a load likewise, and the whole probabilistic model of the study fits in two tables.

3.5. Time-variant loads#

Wind and snow are represented by the distribution of their annual maximum (a Gumbel distribution), the imposed load by that of its five-year maximum. The reliability is required for a 50-year reference period, so the distribution of the maximum over 50 years is needed: for a Gumbel variable with scale \(\sigma\) the standard deviation is unchanged and the mean shifts by \((\sqrt{6}/\pi)\,\sigma \ln(T_2/T_1)\). The characteristic value of a variable load is a fractile of the annual maximum of the product of the load and its model uncertainty \(\Theta_Q\), which is why it is obtained by simulation in the model (The probabilistic model).

3.6. The weighted average and the objective#

The calibration domain is a set of design situations — a failure mode, a dominating variable load, and the proportions \(a_G\) (share of self-weight in the permanent load) and \(a_Q\) (share of the variable load in the total) — each with a weight \(w_k\). Two numbers summarise a set of partial factors: the weighted average reliability

\[ \beta_{\text{w-average}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}) = \sum_k w_k\, \beta_k(\boldsymbol{\gamma}), \]

and the objective

\[ M(\boldsymbol{\gamma}) = \sum_k w_k \big(\beta_k(\boldsymbol{\gamma}) - \beta_t\big)^2 , \]

which the calibration minimises. The first says whether the code is on target on average; the second — or the standard deviation of the \(\beta_k\) — says how uniform the reliability is, which is the more robust indicator of the quality of a code format.
